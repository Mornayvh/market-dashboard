Week in review

US equities fell on concerns about how the AI build-out is being funded. The Nasdaq 100 lost 5.49% to 29,187.55, the S&P 500 1.95% to 7,659.44 and the Russell 2000 3.05% to 2,721.37. The Dow rose 0.67%. Small caps are now about 11% below their August record, which puts them in correction. The cause was credit rather than earnings: CoreWeave's credit default swaps moved to distress levels above 1,100 basis points, its 2032 bonds were quoted near a 15% yield against roughly USD 50bn of debt, and by Friday the high yield market was largely closed to AI borrowers. A report that a partner had refused to fund a USD 10bn data centre in Michigan also pushed Oracle, Nvidia, Broadcom and Alphabet lower.

Credit spreads widened. High yield added 12 basis points to 323, investment grade 3 to 105 and emerging market credit 4 to 159. The widening is concentrated in the weakest borrowers: CCC spreads reached 1,252 basis points on 8 October, up from 807 at the end of September, while BB moved only from 179 to 194. That points to a funding problem at specific heavily indebted companies rather than a general decline in corporate credit quality.

September CPI, released on 14 October, came in at 3.7% against 3.6% expected, with prices up 0.6% on the month. Petrol rose 9.1% and accounted for more than half of that increase. Producer prices were also above forecast. The Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% in September, its first increase since July 2023, and Chair Warsh declined all week to say what it will do at the 27–28 October meeting. Yields still fell: the 10-year down 15 basis points to 5.10% and the 5-year down 12 to 4.90%. The gap between 10-year and 2-year yields narrowed 9 basis points to 0.35%, meaning long-dated bonds rallied more than short-dated ones. Investors were buying government bonds for protection, not because they expect rate cuts. The VIX rose 34.70% to 19.99 and reached 22.31 during Friday's session, its highest in four months.

Brent crude rose 4.91% to 109.86, its highest close since 19 May. At least nine vessels have been attacked near the Strait of Hormuz since 11 October, a missile landed near a Saudi export terminal, and a Ukrainian drone strike shut part of a large Russian refinery. The G7 pledged to release 100 million barrels of diesel and other reserves. Crude is 79.92% higher over twelve months and is the main reason inflation is rising. Gold fell 0.83% to 4,181.10 and is 2.87% lower over twelve months. Silver fell 5.55% to 57.31, including 6% on Wednesday after the CPI release. Copper eased 1.04% to 6.57 but is 32.58% higher over the year on mine supply disruption. Bitcoin fell 1.27% to 81,501 and is 24.67% lower over twelve months.

The JSE All Share rose 2.22% to 110,934.20, the only index on this page to gain, though it is still 1.85% lower over twelve months. The rand strengthened 0.49% to 16.51 and is 4.83% stronger over the year, helped by the SARB's September increase in the repo rate to 7.25% and by firm commodity prices. The Medium-Term Budget Policy Statement is due this week. The dollar index fell 0.36% to 101.84, its first weekly decline in five weeks, and remains 3.51% higher over twelve months. Sterling was the strongest major currency at 1.33, the euro was unchanged at 1.12 and sits at a twelve-month low, and the yen was unchanged at 157.99.

Week in review

Four holdings reported third-quarter results, listed below the commentary. Goldman Sachs earned USD 17.01 a share against USD 13.09 expected, with a return on equity of 18.7%, equities revenue up 72% to USD 6.4bn and record advisory revenue of USD 1.65bn. Morgan Stanley reported a return on tangible equity of 22.6% and record client assets of USD 10.5tn. Bank of America reported USD 0.91 against USD 1.11 expected, but that figure includes a USD 1.2bn regulatory penalty; without it the quarter was close to consensus, although fixed income trading revenue fell 17%. JP Morgan earned USD 15.3bn on revenue up 8%, slightly below consensus, and added USD 539m to loan loss reserves. All four shares are lower over the past month despite these results, because the market is pricing the cost of funding in the quarters ahead rather than the quarter just reported.

The listed private market managers fell further than the banks. KKR lost 12.8% over the month and is 26.1% lower over twelve months, trading at the bottom of its 52-week range. Apollo lost 12.0%. Apollo has capped withdrawals from its retail private credit fund for a second consecutive quarter, with redemption requests reaching 16.8% before easing to 14.7%, and began publishing daily valuations across its credit book on 1 October after the SEC raised concerns about how private credit is valued. It also led a USD 35bn financing package for Broadcom's AI compute platform. These firms are the listed part of the private credit market that funds much of the AI build-out, which is why their shares moved ahead of the banks.

Technology split according to how each company funds its spending. Dell fell 20.5% over the month, including 11.6% on 14 October when Barclays downgraded it and said its enterprise revenue reflects higher memory component costs rather than AI demand. Cerebras fell 35.9% after losing an OpenAI workload to Nvidia and as share lock-ups expired. Palo Alto fell 7.6% on Friday and 18.3% over the week, giving up most of the previous month's gain, after a security flaw was disclosed in its PAN-OS software. Nvidia fell 3.7% over the month; its shipments to China dropped 44% in the first half of the year, reducing its share there to 34% while Huawei reached 28%. Companies funding AI from existing cash did better: Apple closed at a 52-week high and is 39.3% higher over twelve months, Microsoft gained 7.1% over the month and Meta 6.6%.

Berkshire Hathaway closed at a 52-week high after ten consecutive daily gains. The South African and European holdings were steady: Remgro is 19.4% higher over twelve months against a JSE down 1.85%, British American Tobacco rose 6.2% in London and 7.8% in Johannesburg, and Discovery is 23.2% higher over the year with its final dividend paid on 19 October. The Chinese holdings remain weak. Tencent closed at USD 52.75 against a 52-week low of USD 52.72, and the Pentagon has proposed adding Alibaba and BYD to a list of companies alleged to work with the Chinese state.
